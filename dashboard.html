Who says “genocide”? Classification by respondent characteristics
How to read these charts
  • Each dot is the share of people in that group who answered “Yes” to “Would you describe this situation as a genocide?”, with all treatment conditions pooled.
  • The dashed line is the survey-wide rate (). Dots to its right mean the group says “genocide” more often than average.
  • The bar through each dot is a 95% confidence interval. If it does not reach the dashed line, that group differs from the average by more than chance would explain. Faded dots are groups of fewer than 50 people, whose estimates are imprecise.
  • The p-value under each title tests whether the rate differs across that characteristic’s groups at all (χ² test). Below .05, the differences are unlikely to be chance.
  • These are descriptive, not causal. Characteristics overlap (for example, TikTok users skew younger and female), so one pattern can partly reflect another.
Education, income and news attention: why the patterns pull in opposite directions

More-educated and higher-income respondents are less likely to call the situation genocide, while people who follow war news closely are more likely to. Yet education and income go with more news attention, not less, so each pattern partly masks the other. The two charts below separate them.

How to read the arrow charts
  • Each row is one education or income group. The hollow dot is the share saying “genocide” among people in that group who do not follow war news closely; the arrowhead is the share among those who follow it very or extremely closely.
  • The arrow’s length (number at right) is how much close news attention raises “genocide” within that group. Arrows pointing right mean following war news goes with more “genocide” answers.
  • Compare rows to see whether the education or income gap survives among people who follow the news equally closely. The interaction p in each title tests whether the arrows differ in length across groups.
  • Faded rows rest on fewer than 50 people at one end and are imprecise. The middle answer (“somewhat closely”) is left out for clarity.
