## Abstract

What makes an atrocity a genocide? The term genocide is invoked with growing frequency in public discourse even as its legal meaning remains narrow and contested. We argue that citizens employ a "folk" definition that can diverge from law and scholarship in ways that may shape political pressure for action. To identify which informational cues shift these classifications, we field a series of survey-based framing experiments in the United States using news-style vignettes describing an anonymized atrocity. Rather than varying many attributes simultaneously, each experimental module isolates a single conceptual feature—such as perpetrator intent, target group identity, civilian status, severity, or external labeling—embedded within a constant narrative. Our primary outcome measures whether respondents classify the event as genocide, allowing us to estimate how specific frames shift absolute classification thresholds. Secondary outcomes capture confidence in these judgments, moral evaluations, and support for policy responses. By identifying which cues most reliably move respondents across the boundary from "not genocide" to "genocide," this study clarifies how the public operationalizes the concept and when labeling generates pressure for costly international action.

## Introduction

Genocide is viewed as the ultimate crime, the most heinous act of violence against humanity, often referred to as "the crime of crimes." The concept of genocide is increasingly common in discourse on social media, traditional news outlets, political analysts, research, and international relations, spanning both ordinary and elite circles. Individuals, however, use this term differently. First coined by Polish lawyer Raphaël Lemkin in 1941, this word combines the Greek prefix *genos*, meaning race or tribe, and the Latin suffix *cide*, meaning killing. Developed both with a historical lens and as a response to the Nazi's systematic murder of the Jewish people during the Holocaust, the United Nations recognized the crime first in a General Assembly resolution (UN. General Assembly 1946) and then in the 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (hereafter Genocide Convention or the Convention). This Convention, ratified by 154 states, has established the international legal definition, adapted by the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court and numerous other institutions. Despite this legal definition, there is no universally accepted definition; the legal definition deviated from the original conception, and colloquial usage also differs. Therefore, we ask: How do individuals perceive genocide? When is a mass atrocity labeled by the public as a genocide?

Over the last several years, two central events have piqued the US public's interest in the term genocide. The full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine on February 24 2022, brought a sharp increase in the term, reaching a peak by mid-March 2022, and quickly dying down thereafter. The second peak was the Hamas attack against Israel on Oct 7th, 2023, and the full-scale invasion of Gaza by Israeli forces that followed.

It was not necessarily the scale of these events, on a comparative level, that drove the search for the term. Neither the Tigray conflict nor the Sudanese conflict in Darfur create a similar spike, despite outnumbering both the Israeli-Hamas conflict and the Russian-Ukrainian conflict in terms of civilian casualties. Attention to the term genocide, as with more broad attention to conflicts, does not necessarily correlate with the actual scale of the events, but rather in how they are mediated to a Western audience (Asimovic and Esberg 2022).

The court of public opinion operates differently from the evidentiary standards required to prosecute an actor for genocide. Yet, despite this fact, the court of public opinion matters for this process in both domestic courts and international courts (Strezhnev, Simmons and Kim 2019; Wallace 2013; Von Bogdandy, Goldmann and Venzke 2017).

## What is Genocide?

The United Nations was quick to adopt Lemkin's new concept of genocide. After only five years, the young United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 96(1) on "The Crime of Genocide." Affirming that genocide was a violation of international law, the 1946 resolution reads in part:

> Genocide is a denial of the right of existence of entire human groups, as homicidal is the denial of the right to live of individual human beings, such denial of the right of existence shocks the conscience of mankind, results in great losses to humanity in the form of cultural and other contributions represented by these human groups, and is contrary to moral law and to the spirit of the United Nations. Many instances of genocide have occurred when racial, religious, political and other groups have been destroyed, entirely or in part (UN. General Assembly 1946)

Two years later, the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (entered into force in 1951) defined this new legal concept as:

> Genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:
> - (a) Killing members of the group;
> - (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
> - (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
> - (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
> - (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

The Convention not only bans genocide but also broadens states' obligations: "the following acts shall be punishable: genocide; conspiracy to commit genocide; direct and public incitement to commit genocide; complicity in genocide."

The Convention specifies the "intent to destroy, in whole or in part." This phrase has attracted and continues to attract much attention, particularly because of the legal barrier to prove this intent. The Convention narrows potential victim groups down to "national, ethnical, racial, or religious group," leaving outside the political or "other" category listed in the resolution.

There are, and always have been, critiques of this legal definition. Many believe the legal definition is too narrow (Grigore 2025) and raises many problems (Tatz and Higgins 2016). The International Court of Justice has established the "only reasonable inference" standard in *Bosnia v Serbia*, generating much debate among scholars.

Beyond the legal disagreements, these differences lead to important deviations in the determination of genocide across different data sets. The Political Instability Task Force dataset on mass atrocities determines that there were 290 country-years in which a genocide or democide occurred, with 30 unique cases (Harff 2003). On the other hand, the Targeted Mass Killings dataset (Butcher et al. 2020) includes 477 country-years, with 63 unique cases.

## Public Opinion on Genocide: What We Know and Why it Matters

While legal scholars and political scientists discuss the definition and application of the genocide label, the public has actively engaged in these discussions as well, especially since the 7th October attack on Israel and the war that has followed.

Scholars have shown that the public is capable of condemning or justifying violence by both state and non-state actors, but are often biased in how they come to these conclusions (Manekin and Mitts 2022; Wasow 2020; Edwards and Arnon 2021). The broad consensus that emerges from these series of studies examining perceptions of violence is that in-group biases shape the perceptions of actors in important ways, but more importantly that more violence leads to stronger condemnation of the actors engaged in it. Cordell (2025) finds that in the US partisan politics shapes perceptions of perpetrators more than other group identities in the US, such as race, religion, and citizenship.

We broadly expect that these processes of perceptions of political violence would translate into the world of mass atrocities, within each specific cultural-political context with their respective in-group biases.

## What makes an atrocity a genocide?

Public understandings of genocide are unlikely to map cleanly onto the legal definition codified in the United Nations Genocide Convention. Instead, individuals are likely to rely on a "folk" definition shaped by intuitive judgments about intent, victim identity, the nature of violence, and cues from authoritative actors. We derive a set of hypotheses about how specific informational features of an atrocity influence whether individuals apply the genocide label.

**Hypothesis 1 (Intent):** Explicit statements indicating that perpetrators seek to eliminate or destroy a population will increase the likelihood that respondents classify the event as genocide.

**Hypothesis 2 (Group Targeting):** Describing violence as directed at a specific social group will increase the likelihood that respondents classify the event as genocide, relative to describing it as directed at the broader population.

**Hypothesis 3 (Civilian Targeting):** Describing victims as non-violent civilians will increase the likelihood that respondents classify the event as genocide, relative to describing them as militants.

**Hypothesis 4 (Severity and Tactics):** More severe or systematic forms of violence will increase the likelihood that respondents classify the event as genocide.

**Hypothesis 5 (Source Credibility):** The effect of labeling an event as genocide will be larger when a label is applied by an authoritative source.

**Hypothesis 6 (Labeling):** Explicitly labeling an event as genocide will increase the likelihood that respondents classify the event as genocide.

**Hypothesis 7 (Interaction Effects):** The combined presence of multiple genocide-relevant cues will increase the likelihood of genocide classification more than the sum of their individual effects.

## Research Design

We field a series of parallel vignette experiments to identify the conditions under which members of the public classify an atrocity as genocide. We employ a single-profile design rather than a paired comparison format like a conjoint experiment. We are not interested in which atrocity is more genocidal than another. Rather, we seek to identify the conditions under which members of the public apply the genocide label at all. This is a problem of threshold classification, not relative preference.

We present each vignette as a stand-alone news-style narrative describing an ongoing atrocity in an anonymized country. Each vignette is presented under a neutral, fictional news header (e.g., *World News Today*) with a date and international desk label.

All vignettes follow a common narrative template:

> Reports from local residents and independent observers indicate that the government of Country B has carried out a campaign against its people in parts of the country over the last six months.
>
> Reports indicate that *[Treatment condition: Intent, Severity, Targets]*
>
> Local organizations report that individuals and communities in affected areas have experienced major disruptions to daily life. Many people struggle to access basic goods and face difficulties moving safely from one area to another.
>
> Civil society organizations are urging restraint and the protection of civilians. Public debate continues over how to characterize the situation in Country B and what actions, if any, the international community should take.
>
> *[Treatment condition: Labeling]*

During the experiment, we require participants to answer two attention questions. Participants who fail one, or both, of these attention checks will be excluded from the analysis.

### Experiment #1: Intent

Our first experiment focuses on whether intent is stated by the perpetrator of the violence described in the vignette. We therefore vary whether perpetrators: (1) make no clear statement about intent, (2) explicitly articulate a goal of destroying the group, or denies genocidal intent, either by (3a) only stating a denial or by (3b) explaining that the campaign is designed to thwart a security threat.

#### Table 2: Experiment 1 — Intent Treatment Wordings

| Condition | Text Inserted in Vignette |
|---|---|
| No intent provided | No additional information about the government's intent is provided. |
| Government denies intent | Leaders deny targeting any particular group. |
| Government denies intent and explains on security grounds | Leaders deny targeting any particular group and explain that it is being done to thwart attacks by an imminent security threat. |
| Explicit intent to destroy | Leaders explicitly state a goal of destroying the group. |

---

### Experiment #2: Tactic and Severity

Our second experiment focuses explicitly on the violence itself — in terms of its form and severity. We vary the reported number of people affected (hundreds vs. tens of thousands) and we vary the types of violence: targeted killings, sexual violence, or destruction of infrastructure. We have a total of 7 conditions, for each combination of violence and severity, and a true control condition.

#### Table 3: Experiment 2 — Tactic and Severity Treatment Wordings

| Condition | Text Inserted in Vignette |
|---|---|
| No tactic or severity specified | No additional details about the methods or scale of the campaign are provided. |
| Killing - Low | Reports indicate that the campaign has involved targeted killings, affecting hundreds of individuals. |
| Killing - High | Reports indicate that the campaign has involved targeted killings, affecting tens of thousands of individuals. |
| Sexual violence - Low | Reports indicate that the campaign has involved widespread sexual violence, affecting hundreds of individuals. |
| Sexual violence - High | Reports indicate that the campaign has involved widespread sexual violence, affecting tens of thousands of individuals. |
| Infrastructure - Low | Reports indicate that the campaign has involved the destruction of buildings, food stores, and water systems, affecting hundreds of individuals. |
| Infrastructure - High | Reports indicate that the campaign has involved the destruction of buildings, food stores, and water systems, affecting tens of thousands of individuals. |

---

### Experiment #3: Group Identity and Use of Violence

Our third experiment tests whether the identity, and political activities, of the targeted population significantly shift genocide evaluations. We vary two conditions: (1) group type (political opposition vs. racial minority), and (2) whether the group engaged in violence (militant vs. non-violent). As a control, we use a generic description of "civilians" as the target group.

#### Table 4: Experiment 3 — Target Group and Activity Treatment Wordings

| Condition | Text Inserted in Vignette |
|---|---|
| No group specified | The campaign is described as targeting civilians in affected areas. |
| Political opposition - militant | The campaign targets members of a political opposition group who are described as engaging in violent activities against the government. |
| Political opposition - non-violent | The campaign targets members of a political opposition group who are described as engaging in non-violent activities. |
| Racial minority - militant | The campaign targets members of a racial minority group who are described as engaging in violent activities against the government. |
| Racial minority - non-violent | The campaign targets members of a racial minority group who are described as engaging in non-violent activities. |

---

### Experiment #4: Organization and Labeling

Last, we vary whether external actors label the event and how they characterize it. The labeling attribute includes three levels: no label, characterization as serious human rights abuses, or explicit designation as genocide. We also randomize whether the label is attributed to the United Nations, the United States, or international human rights organizations. This results in a total of seven conditions.

#### Table 5: Experiment 4 — External Labeling Treatment Wordings

| Condition | Text Inserted in Vignette |
|---|---|
| No external characterization | No organization has publicly characterized the situation. |
| United Nations – human rights abuses | In a recent statement, the United Nations described the situation as "serious human rights abuses." |
| United Nations – genocide | In a recent statement, the United Nations described the situation as "genocide." |
| International HRO – human rights abuses | In a recent statement, international human rights organizations described the situation as "serious human rights abuses." |
| International HRO – genocide | In a recent statement, international human rights organizations described the situation as "genocide." |
| United States – human rights abuses | In a recent statement, the United States government described the situation as "serious human rights abuses." |
| United States – genocide | In a recent statement, the United States government described the situation as "genocide." |

## Power Calculations

We conduct power calculations to determine the sample size required to detect substantively meaningful differences in the probability that respondents classify an event as genocide. Because the primary outcome is binary, we base our calculations on standard formulas for differences in proportions under a two-sided test with α = 0.05 and 80% power.

We assume a baseline probability of 0.5, which provides a conservative estimate of variance. Under these assumptions, detecting a difference of 10 percentage points requires approximately 392 observations in a two-arm comparison, while detecting differences of 8 and 6 percentage points requires approximately 613 and 1,089 observations, respectively.

### Table 9: Sample Size Requirements by Experimental Module

| Experiment | Arms | 10-pt Effect | 8-pt Effect | 6-pt Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intent | 3 | 676 | 1,057 | 1,879 |
| Organization × Labeling | 7 | 1,578 | 2,467 | 4,383 |
| Target Group × Victim Status | 5 | 1,127 | 1,762 | 3,131 |
| Tactic × Severity | 7 | 1,578 | 2,467 | 4,383 |

*Notes: Entries report total required sample sizes (including a 15% buffer for attrition) to detect differences in the probability of classifying an event as genocide. Calculations assume 80% power, a two-sided α = 0.05, and a baseline probability of 0.5.*

## Survey Instrument

### Introduction

You will be asked to read short descriptions of situations occurring in another country and answer questions about your impressions. Some of the content may involve descriptions of violence or conflict. You may stop participation at any time without penalty.

### Pre-Treatment Questions

1. How closely do you follow international news?
   - Not at all / A little / Somewhat / Very closely

2. How often do you get news from the following sources?
   - Television news: Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often
   - Newspapers (online or print): Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often
   - Social media: Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often

3. Which social media platforms do you use regularly? (Select all that apply)
   - Facebook / Twitter/X / Instagram / TikTok / YouTube / Other / None

### Main Post-Vignette Questions

1. In your opinion, would you describe the situation you read about as genocide? *(Yes / No)*

2. How confident are you in your answer? *(Not at all confident / Slightly confident / Moderately confident / Very confident / Extremely confident)*

3. Do you believe governments have an obligation to respond to situations like the one(s) described? *(Yes / No)*

4. Should governments respond in the following ways?
   - Diplomatic pressure: Yes / No
   - Economic sanctions: Yes / No
   - Military intervention: Yes / No
   - Humanitarian intervention: Yes / No
   - Legal action: Yes / No

### Additional Post-Treatment Questions

1. Please indicate how much you agree or disagree with the following statement: *The term "genocide" should be reserved only for the very worst, most extreme cases of mass violence.* *(Strongly disagree → Strongly agree)*

2. How much do you agree or disagree with the following statements?
   - (a) Calling a situation "genocide" should commit countries to stronger action.
   - (b) Countries have a moral obligation to intervene when genocide is occurring.
   - (c) The term "genocide" is often used symbolically rather than to prompt action.
   - (d) Governments should avoid using the term "genocide" unless they are prepared to act.
   *(Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither agree nor disagree / Agree / Strongly agree)*

3. Were you thinking about any real-world event while reading the scenario(s)? *(Yes / No / Not sure)*

4. If yes, what event were you thinking about? *(Open-ended free response)*

5. In your own words, what makes an event a "genocide" rather than another form of violence? What makes genocide unique? *(Open-ended free response)*
